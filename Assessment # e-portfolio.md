## E-Portfolio: Automated Decision Making in Government Systems
## Introduction to Automated Decision-Making

Automated Decision-Making (ADM) refers to the use of computer systems to make decisions or assist in making decisions that would otherwise involve human judgement. These systems analyse information, provide recommendations, or make decisions using predefined rules, statistical models, machine learning, and artificial intelligence (AI).

ADM can be applied to social security, eligibility checks, compliance activities, and the delivery of government services.

ADM can improve the efficiency, consistency, and speed of government services. However, it can also create risks involving unfair outcomes, inaccurate information, privacy violations, limited transparency, and difficulties challenging decisions. These risks are particularly significant when automated decisions affect people's income, access to services, legal rights, and wellbeing.

As an aspiring ICT professional, I need to consider not only whether a system works technically but also whether it is fair, accountable, transparent, explainable, and designed around the people affected by it.

The following six artefacts examine these issues in Australia, Canada, and the United Kingdom. They also demonstrate how ethical theories and professional responsibilities can support responsible automated decision-making.

## Artefact 1 — Australian Government: Robodebt Implementation Update
<img width="940" height="770" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/cb99a566-ce03-4dc3-9a9a-f1dca5026d71" />


**Source:** [Robodebt Royal Commission Implementation Update](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/robodebt-royal-commission.pdf)

### Summary of the Artefact

The Australian Government's November 2024 implementation update discusses progress on recommendations from the Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme. It addresses improvements to government services, the circumstances of vulnerable recipients, documentation, and consultation.

The report highlights that government automation should consider the circumstances and experiences of affected individuals rather than being treated purely as a technical or administrative process (Australian Government 2024).

### Justification for Selecting the Artefact

I selected this artefact because Robodebt is a significant Australian case study demonstrating the harm that can occur when government decision-making processes lack adequate safeguards.

It relates to **utilitarianism**, as the consequences of automated systems should be evaluated according to their effects on individuals and society. It also relates to **Kantian ethics**, because citizens should be treated with dignity rather than merely as records to be processed.

From an ICT professional's perspective, this case demonstrates the importance of the Australian Computer Society (ACS) principle of public interest. It highlights why fairness and accountability must be considered alongside efficiency when designing and implementing automated systems.

**Source:** [National Framework for the Assurance of Artificial Intelligence in Government](https://www.finance.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-06/National-framework-for-the-assurance-of-AI-in-government.pdf)

### Summary of the Artefact

The National Framework for the Assurance of Artificial Intelligence in Government provides guidance for the responsible use of AI within Australian government agencies.

The framework connects AI ethics principles with governance and assurance measures. It addresses automated decision-making, administrative law, privacy, governance, transparency, accountability, and ongoing quality assurance (Australian Government 2024).

### Justification for Selecting the Artefact

I selected this artefact because it demonstrates that responsible AI implementation requires practical procedures for identifying, assessing, and managing risks rather than relying solely on good intentions.

It relates to **utilitarianism** because assurance activities can help identify and reduce potential harm before an AI system affects a large number of people. It also demonstrates professional responsibility through documented checks, risk assessments, and accountability measures.

As an aspiring ICT professional, I learned that ethical considerations must be incorporated throughout the development and deployment lifecycle. Technical testing, risk assessment, and accountability should be integrated into system design and implementation.

## Artefact 3 — Australian Human Rights Commission: Use of Automated Decision-Making by Government

**Source:** [Use of Automated Decision-Making by Government](https://humanrights.gov.au/__data/assets/file/0023/71834/Use-of-Automated-Decision-Making-by-Government_0.pdf)

### Summary of the Artefact

The Australian Human Rights Commission's submission discusses human rights safeguards for automated decision-making by government agencies.

It identifies measures such as conducting human rights impact assessments before implementation, notifying individuals when automated decision-making materially affects them, providing reasons for decisions, and establishing adequate processes for challenging decisions.

The submission also considers the importance of oversight and the risks of introducing new technologies without sufficient safeguards (Australian Human Rights Commission 2025).

### Justification for Selecting the Artefact

I selected this artefact because it places the rights and experiences of individuals at the centre of technology governance.

It relates to **Kantian ethics**, which emphasises treating people as individuals with dignity rather than merely as a means to achieve organisational objectives. It also reflects the ACS principle of public interest because government systems can affect people's rights and access to essential services.

This artefact helped me understand that transparency must be meaningful. Individuals should be able to understand when automated decision-making has been used and have a fair opportunity to challenge decisions that affect them.

It demonstrates the importance of combining technical accountability with respect for human rights.

## Artefact 4 — Australian Government: Standard for AI Transparency Statements
<img width="940" height="494" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/499e1366-2e51-49eb-89ff-dc5b52aaddd6" />

**Source:** [Standard for AI Transparency Statements](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/standard-ai-transparency-statements)

### Summary of the Artefact

The Australian Government's Standard for AI Transparency Statements provides guidance for government agencies to publish information about their use of AI through AI transparency statements.

It establishes expectations for explaining the purpose of AI use, the nature of its applications, potentially impactful uses, and the monitoring of AI systems.

The standard aims to build public confidence by making information about government AI use clear and accessible (Digital Transformation Agency 2025).

### Justification for Selecting the Artefact

I selected this artefact because it demonstrates how transparency can improve public understanding of technology used by government institutions.

It relates to **Kantian ethics** because individuals deserve respect and understandable explanations about systems that may affect them. It also connects with the ACS principle of honesty through clear communication about technology and its limitations.

From a utilitarian perspective, transparency can help identify risks and support improvements before widespread harm occurs.

This artefact taught me that responsible ICT professionals should communicate in accessible language rather than relying only on technical terminology. Clear explanations, ongoing monitoring, and accountability are essential when implementing AI systems.

## Artefact 5 — Canada: Directive on Automated Decision-Making
<img width="940" height="602" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f0ec1bde-0724-40dc-b3f1-f6f538b30eda" />


**Source:** [Government of Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/automated-decision-making.html)

### Summary of the Artefact

The Canadian Government's Directive on Automated Decision-Making establishes requirements for the responsible use of automated decision systems in government.

It addresses transparency, accountability, lawfulness, and procedural fairness. It also includes an Algorithmic Impact Assessment to help government departments identify risks and determine appropriate requirements before systems enter production (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat 2025).

### Justification for Selecting the Artefact

I selected this artefact because it provides an international perspective that can be compared with Australia's approach to automated decision-making.

It relates to **utilitarianism** because impact assessments can help identify and minimise potential harm to the public before it occurs. It also connects with **Kantian ethics** through its emphasis on procedural fairness and accountability.

This artefact helped me understand that responsible automation requires institutional policies, documented risk assessments, and ongoing review. The responsibility does not belong to developers alone; government organisations must also establish appropriate governance and oversight.

It also encouraged me to consider how international governance approaches can inform responsible ICT practices in Australia.

## Artefact 6 — United Kingdom: Review into Bias in Algorithmic Decision-Making

**Source:** [Review into Bias in Algorithmic Decision-Making](https://www.gov.uk/data-ethics-guidance/review-into-bias-in-algorithmic-decision-making)

### Summary of the Artefact

The UK Government's review examines potential bias in algorithmic decision-making, including systems used to make decisions about individuals.

It considers the risk of unfair bias and the role of data and risk-management techniques in supporting fairer outcomes. The review covers areas including recruitment, financial services, policing, and local government (UK Government 2025).

### Justification for Selecting the Artefact

I selected this artefact because it demonstrates that algorithmic bias is a concern across different countries and areas of society.

Automated decisions can affect important opportunities and services in many contexts. It relates to **utilitarianism** because reducing unequal outcomes can help prevent harm and improve societal wellbeing. It also relates to **Kantian ethics**, as individuals should be treated fairly rather than subjected to discriminatory assumptions or biased data patterns.

This artefact helped me understand that evaluating an algorithm only by its overall accuracy is insufficient. ICT professionals must also examine how systems perform across different groups and identify potential unfair outcomes throughout the system lifecycle.

## Overall Reflection

These six artefacts have improved my understanding of the ethical and professional responsibilities associated with automated decision-making.

I learned that automated systems can improve the efficiency and consistency of government services, but they can also create serious risks involving privacy, fairness, transparency, accountability, and human rights.

The Robodebt case demonstrates the importance of effective safeguards, while the Australian AI assurance framework highlights the need for structured risk management. The Australian Human Rights Commission's submission and the AI transparency standard further demonstrate the importance of protecting individual rights and providing understandable information.

The Canadian directive and the UK review show that these challenges extend beyond Australia and that international approaches can provide useful lessons for responsible technology governance.

Through utilitarianism, I can evaluate the potential benefits and harms of a system for individuals and society. Through Kantian ethics, I can consider whether a system respects people's dignity and rights. These perspectives can guide my decisions as an aspiring ICT professional.

In future projects, I aim to consider fairness, transparency, privacy, accountability, and public interest throughout the system development lifecycle rather than focusing exclusively on technical performance.
